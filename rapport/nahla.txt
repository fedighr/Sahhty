Introduction
Since Internet usage has spread to virtually every aspect of daily life, health-related applications have grown exponentially. According to the WHO, the use of mobile wireless technologies for health purposes — through applications known as mHealth — not only gives users access to information, services, and skills, but also contributes to positive changes in health behaviours in order to prevent certain acute or chronic diseases [1]. This use is all the more beneficial given that, still according to the WHO, there are more than 7 billion mobile phone subscriptions worldwide, and that in some countries, access to wireless technologies is easier and more affordable than access to clean water and electricity [2].
mHealth applications are installed on small connected devices such as mobile phones, smartwatches, or other wearable devices. In their simplified versions, they primarily offer monitoring of the user's physical activity, such as distance travelled, step count, calories burned, and a few other metrics such as heart rate or sleep cycles. These applications generally collect data without any input from the user. Other applications require the user to manually enter additional parameters, such as dietary habits or medical data that mobile devices are not capable of measuring on their own. The combination of automatic data collection and user input allows these applications to offer various behavioural interventions that can help users lead a healthier lifestyle and reduce the likelihood of preventable health problems [3].
Alongside these applications, which are generally free and accessible to all, other applications are managed by healthcare and hospital institutions and are intended exclusively for their patients. They allow users to manage their appointments with the various practitioners working within the institution. In these systems, the medical record is fully digitised, and the patient receives through the application the results of biological tests, radiological examinations, and other investigations, as well as their prescriptions. Through the institution's network, each practitioner has access to the patient's complete medical record, including medical history, treatments, and all information relating to the patient's prior health status. In such fully digitalised and centralised systems, each practitioner has a comprehensive overview of their patients, which undeniably prevents the unnecessary repetition of already-performed tests and avoids drug interactions and overdoses that can occur when all data is not available.
In healthcare systems such as the Tunisian system, where patient medical records are not fully computerised and where there is little or no communication between different healthcare structures, patients often find themselves carrying large folders containing a disorganised mix of biological test results, various radiological imaging reports, and prescriptions from different practitioners. This multiplicity of healthcare providers, combined with the medical nomadism of some patients, can lead at best to redundancies — repeated requests for the same tests or radiological examinations — and at worst to drug interactions or even overdoses. This not only causes a surge in healthcare expenditure but also poses real risks to patient health, with significant social and economic consequences for the community.
People living with chronic diseases are particularly vulnerable to this situation. Between ongoing chronic treatment and its constraints — number of daily doses, regular check-ups, and lifestyle measures — and occasional treatments, drug interactions are very often either overlooked or ignored, and the effects on physiological or biological parameters are not always taken into account.
While this problem affects all categories of individuals, whether chronically or occasionally ill, it takes on a different dimension when it concerns a pregnant woman, since it involves not only the health of the expectant mother but also that of the unborn child.
Based on this observation, this work proposes to dedicate an application to pregnant women, enabling them to manage their health and share all their medical data with the various healthcare providers they will encounter throughout their pregnancy. Named Sahhty — meaning "my health" in Tunisian Arabic — this application will allow the pregnant woman to monitor her pregnancy from the day it is discovered until the moment of delivery. In addition to her biological data and physiological parameters such as weight and blood pressure, the application offers the possibility of entering all current medications, with the ability to detect drug interactions and teratogenic substances.

Data Collection
For data cross-referencing, several publicly available databases were used. As the application is intended in its first phase for Tunisian users, the list of medications marketed in Tunisia was retrieved from the DPM (Directorate of Pharmacy and Medicines) website. The list of medications reimbursed by the CNAM (National Health Insurance Fund) was also used. For drug interactions, data from the Thériaque database was used. For medications with teratogenic risk, the Lecrat website was consulted.
