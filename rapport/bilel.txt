General Introduction

The rapid growth of digital platforms has significantly transformed the service economy,
making it easier and faster for users to find and access services. Today, people can dis-
cover, compare, and request services online with just a few clicks. However, despite these
advancements, the service marketplace remains fragmented. Users often need to switch be-
tween multiple platforms to meet different needs, each with its own rules, pricing systems,
and limited communication features. At the same time, service providers face challenges in
gaining visibility and reaching a wider audience across different types of services.

This fragmentation is even more noticeable in emerging digital markets, where existing
solutions are not always adapted to local needs. As a result, there is a clear gap between
what users expect and what current platforms can offer. This situation highlights the need
for a unified and intelligent service marketplace that can bring together different types of
services, support both local and remote interactions, and provide a more seamless and effi-
cient user experience.

This project was carried out at Satisfy Insight, a Tunisian startup specializing in data-
driven technological solutions. It was conducted as part of a final-year internship in partial
fulfillment of the Bachelor’s degree in Computer Science, specializing in Software Engineer-
ing, at the Higher Institute of Sciences and Technology of Hammam Sousse (ESSTHS).

The internship lasted three months, from February 1 to April 30, and allowed the ap-
plication of academic knowledge through a real-world project. Khadamni is an integrated
service marketplace designed to unify various service interactions within a single place.
The platform addresses a simple yet ambitious question: what would it look like if all service
interactions local and remote, across all categories could happen in one place, in real time, with the
intelligence and flexibility that modern users expect?

The development of Khadamni follows an Agile approach based on iterative sprints.
The platform is built using modern technologies, including React and Vite for the frontend,
FastAPI for the backend, MongoDB for data management, and WebSocket for real-time com-
munication.

This report presents the full development process. Chapter 1 introduces the project con-
text and methodology. Chapter 2 details the system requirements. Chapter 3 presents the
system design using the C4 model. Chapter 4 describes the implementation, and Chapter 5
covers testing and validation.

Overall, this work aims to deliver a modern solution that improves service interactions

while demonstrating a structured software engineering approach.

1.3 Project Context

1.3.1 General Context

The way people find services has changed dramatically over the last decade. Where people
once relied on word of mouth or physical directories, they now turn to their phones, expect-
ing to find a plumber, an electrician, or a delivery service in under two minutes. Digital
platforms have made this possible, but not without friction.

The market is fragmented. A user who needs a plumber today, a cleaner tomorrow, and
a moving company next week must navigate three different platforms, create three different
accounts, and hope that each provider is trustworthy. For providers, the situation is not
much better: visibility is limited, communication is restricted, and price negotiation is often
either absent or one-sided.

This fragmentation does not only affect physical, in-person services. A growing segment
of service providers work entirely remotely -tutors, graphic designers, IT consultants, and
translators -and they face the same problem in reverse: the platforms that host them are
either too niche or too crowded, leaving them with poor visibility and no way to connect
with local clients who might prefer a hybrid or online arrangement.

The question Khadamni sets out to answer is simple: what would it look like if all of these
interactions local and remote, across all service categories happened in one place, in real time, with
the flexibility of a direct conversation?

1.3.2 Formal Problem Statement

Despite the proliferation of online service platforms, users continue to face a set of recurring
and unresolved problems:

• Fragmented service discovery: Most platforms specialize in a single service category
or job type. Users searching for diverse services or a mix of in-person and remote
providers must maintain accounts on multiple platforms, increasing friction and re-
ducing efficiency.

• No unified space for local and remote services: Platforms that handle location-based
services typically ignore remote workers, and platforms for freelancers typically ignore
proximity. There is no single environment where a client can find a nearby plumber
and a remote translator in the same search.

• Limited real-time interaction: Communication between clients and providers is often
asynchronous, delayed, or mediated through rigid request forms. There is rarely a
space for genuine negotiation or clarification.

• Weak trust mechanisms: Some platforms require sensitive identity documents dur-
ing registration without clear data protection guarantees. Others provide insufficient
information about provider reliability, making selection feel uncertain.

• Inflexible pricing and cancellation: Fixed-price models or opaque fee structures leave
users with little control. Cancellation policies are often punitive and poorly communi-
cated.

Because of these limitations, users still lack a unified and flexible platform where they
can easily find different services in one place, communicate directly with providers, and
choose them with confidence.

Structural Evaluation: The TRUST Methodology

To quantify the gaps in current solutions, we applied the TRUST method. This framework
measures the maturity of a marketplace across five critical dimensions:

• T - Transparency: Clarity of pricing, service information, and process visibility.

• R - Reliability: The platform’s ability to ensure dependable service delivery.

• U - User Control: Flexibility given to users in negotiation and decision-making.

• S - Security: Strength of identity verification and data protection mechanisms.

• T - Traceability: Ability to track service history, communications, and transactions.

Each criterion in the TRUST framework is evaluated using a binary score: 1 when the feature
is clearly supported, and 0 when it is weak or absent. This approach ensures a simple and
consistent comparison across all platforms, as summarized in Table 1.1.

3.1

Introduction

In this chapter, we present the architectural design and modeling of the Khadamni plat-
form.It provides a structured and progressive view of the system, describing its organiza-
tion, components, and interactions.
The objective is to move from a high-level perspective to more detailed technical represen-
tations, ensuring clarity and coherence.

To support this approach, the C4 model is adopted as a modern and relatively new ar-
chitectural modeling approach, enabling the system to be described across multiple levels
of abstraction.

3.2 Modeling Methodology

The modeling of the Khadamnicplatform follows a structured and hierarchical approach,
representing the system across multiple levels of abstraction. This enables both technical
and non-technical stakeholders to progressively understand the system, from a global view
to detailed design elements.
In this process , we adopted a top-down strategy, starting from an overall representation and
gradually refining it into more detailed views, ensuring clarity, consistency, and alignment
across perspectives.
In parallel, the design is guided by Clean Architecture principles, organizing the system into
layers such as controllers, services, and repositories. This separation improves maintainabil-
ity, scalability, and testability, resulting in a clear and well-structured architecture.

3.2.1 Limitations of Traditional Modeling Approaches

Traditional modeling techniques, particularly UML (Unified Modeling Language), have
long been widely adopted in software engineering due to their ability to represent differ-
ent aspects of a system through various diagram types such as class, sequence, and use case
diagrams. However, when applied to modern software systems, these approaches reveal
several limitations.

38

Chapter 3. System Design and Modeling

39

One of the main challenges lies in the large number of available diagram types, which can
introduce unnecessary complexity and lead to inconsistent modeling practices. In addition,
UML diagrams tend to focus either on high-level abstractions or low-level implementation
details, without providing a clear and continuous transition between the levels. This lack of
hierarchical structuring makes it difficult to maintain a coherent overall view of the system.

Furthermore, traditional modeling techniques were not specifically designed for mod-
ern architectures such as distributed systems, web applications, or service-oriented envi-
ronments. As a result, they often struggle to represent these systems in an intuitive and
meaningful way.

Finally, UML diagrams can become overloaded with technical details, reducing their

readability and limiting their accessibility to non-technical stakeholders.

These limitations highlight the need for a more structured, scalable, and accessible mod-

eling approach, better suited to the complexity of contemporary software systems.

3.2.2 Overview of the C4 Model

The C4 model is a modern approach to software architecture visualization, focusing on clar-
ity, simplicity, and consistency. It organizes the system into four hierarchical levels:

• System Context: provides a high-level view of the system and its interactions with

external actors.

• Container: describes the main system building blocks and their responsibilities.

• Component: details the internal structure of each container.

• Code: represents the implementation-level structure of the system.

Each level refines the previous one, enabling a progressive understanding of the system

from a global perspective to detailed technical elements, as illustrated in Figure 3.1.

3.5.6 Interactions Between Components

The data flow within the Backend API follows a strict top-down layered pattern:

• The Frontend Application sends REST requests to the appropriate Controller based

• Each Controller delegates execution to its corresponding Service: the Auth Controller

queries directed at the Database container.

• In parallel, three services maintain outbound integrations with external systems: the
Auth Service triggers email delivery via SMTP; the Provider Service queries Nomi-
natim for geocoding and OSRM for routing; and the Request Service submits request
descriptions to the Groq AI inference engine and processes the structured responses it
returns.

This three-layer internal organization Controllers, Services, Repository reflects a delib-
erate application of the separation of concerns principle. It ensures that HTTP handling,
business logic, and data access remain independently testable and modifiable, providing a
solid architectural foundation for the continued development and scaling of the Khadamni
platform.

4.4 AI Integration

4.4.1 Overview

Khadamni integrates artificial intelligence through Groq, a cloud inference platform that
runs the Llama 3.3-70B large language model. The AI is not a standalone feature - it is
embedded into two core workflows of the platform: helping clients describe what they need,
and helping moderators resolve disputes between users. Both features work by sending text
to Groq and receiving a structured response that the backend then uses to take action.

4.4.2 Feature 1 : Automatic Request Analysis

When a client wants to request a service, they describe their problem in their own words,
Instead of forcing him to navigate dropdowns and categories
even the Tunisian dialect.
manually, the platform sends that description to the AI and lets it figure out the rest.

What happens step by step:

1. The client types a free-text description of their problem (example: "3and¯ı problema fil

robiniyya")

2. The frontend sends the description to the backend via POST /api/ai/analyze

3. The backend constructs a prompt and sends it to the Groq API

4. Llama 3.3-70B reads the description and returns a structured JSON response contains:

• The detected service category (example: Plumbing)

• An estimated price range based on the type of job

• A confidence level for the detected category

5. The backend returns this result to the frontend

6. The client sees the suggested category and price, and can confirm or adjust before

submitting

Why this matters: Without AI, the client would need to manually know which category
their problem belongs to. With AI, they just describe the problem in any language or di-
alect and the platform handles the classification automatically. This significantly lowers the
barrier for non-technical users.

4.4.3 Feature 2 : AI-Assisted Dispute Mediation

When a conflict arises between a client and a provider - for example, a disagreement over
whether the job was completed correctly - either party can file a dispute report. The moder-
ator then needs to review it and make a decision. To help with this, the platform uses AI to
analyze the full conversation and produce an independent assessment before the moderator
even opens the report.

Chapter 4. Solution implementation and evaluation

69

What happens step by step:

1. A user submits a dispute report through the platform

2. The backend retrieves the complete chat transcript between the client and the provider

3. A structured prompt is built containing the transcript and the context of the job

4. The prompt is sent to Groq - Llama 3.3-70B reads the full conversation and produces a

report containing:

• A summary of what happened

• A recommendation - in favor of the client, in favor of the provider, or neutral

• The key evidence from the conversation that supports the recommendation

• A suggested action for the moderator to take

5. The AI report is saved in the reports collection in MongoDB

6. The moderator sees the AI analysis directly on their dashboard before making a final

decision

Why this matters: Without AI, the moderator would need to read through every mes-
sage in the conversation manually to understand what happened. The AI pre-processes the
conversation and highlights the important parts, saving time and reducing the chance of
a biased or uninformed decision. The moderator always makes the final call - the AI only
provides a recommendation.

4.4.4 Provider Recommendation Scoring

In addition to the two main AI features, the platform uses a scoring formula to rank providers
when a client searches for nearby help. The score is calculated on the backend using the fol-
lowing formula:

Score = 0.6 × Distance Score + 0.4 × Quality Score

• Distance Score how close the provider is to the client’s location (closer = higher score)

• Quality Score based on the provider’s average rating from past jobs

Providers are sorted by this score so that the most relevant result , a nearby provider
with a good reputation appears at the top of the list. The Groq API can also be called to
generate a natural language explanation of why a particular provider was recommended, as

5.9 Performance Testing

5.9.1 Objective

Performance testing evaluates how the system behaves under varying levels of concurrent
load, measuring response times, throughput, error rates, and real-time feature latency. The
objective is to identify the platform’s operational limits and confirm that response times
meet the thresholds defined in NFR-1 and NFR-2.

5.9.2 Performance Testing Tool

All load and stress tests were conducted using Locust 2.43, an open-source Python-based
load testing framework. Test scenarios were written as Python classes simulating realistic
user behavior across all major API endpoints. Each virtual user alternates between provider
search, request management, chat history retrieval, and AI analysis calls, with a wait time
of 1–3 seconds between actions to simulate realistic browsing patterns.

stance backed by MongoDB, with external API calls routed over a 20 Mbps fiber connec-

• Async backend performance: FastAPI with Motor handles concurrent requests with-

• Stable real-time communication: WebSocket connections remain reliable across chat,

GPS tracking, and price negotiation with latency under 800ms

• Effective access control: All route guards correctly enforce role restrictions on both

frontend and API layers

• AI dialect handling: Groq integration successfully classifies service categories from

Chapter 5. Tests and Validation

• Geospatial query efficiency: MongoDB 2dsphere index delivers provider search re-

5.10.5 Limitations and Recommendations

• Groq rate limits: The free-tier plan rejects all concurrent AI requests, causing 100%
failure under parallel load (PERF-005); upgrading to a paid plan or implementing a
sequential request queue is required for production

• Single-worker deployment: Stress testing revealed connection refusal beyond 200–
300 concurrent users on a single Uvicorn worker; deploying with multiple workers or
behind a load balancer is recommended for production

• Limited automated E2E coverage: End-to-end scenarios were validated manually; im-
plementing Cypress or Playwright would enable automated regression for full user
journeys

• Local file storage: Uploaded images are stored on disk; migrating to a cloud solution

(S3, Cloudinary) is necessary for production scalability

5.11 Conclusion

This chapter presented the comprehensive testing and validation strategy applied to the
Khadamni platform, structured around six complementary testing levels. Unit tests con-
firmed the correctness of isolated backend functions using PBKDF2-SHA256 password hash-
ing, with a real pytest suite providing automated regression protection across 12 test cases.
Functional tests systematically validated all 21 functional requirements defined in Chapter 2
through a complete traceability matrix. Integration tests verified the correct communication
between all system layers and confirmed the platform’s resilience to external service failures
-including Groq AI timeouts, Nominatim unavailability, and OSRM routing failures -with
graceful fallback mechanisms in each case. Security tests validated access control, OTP life-
cycle enforcement, JWT protection, and suspension mechanics through detailed scenario-
based testing, all passing against their corresponding NFR targets. Performance tests, con-
ducted using Locust 2.43, established that core API endpoints respond in under 15ms under
200 concurrent users, with geospatial queries averaging 6ms. The system’s degradation
threshold was identified at approximately 200–300 concurrent users on a single-worker de-
ployment, and the Groq free-tier AI endpoint was confirmed unable to handle concurrent
requests -a known external limitation.

The quantitative metrics demonstrate a 98.1% pass rate across 52 test cases, 100% re-
quirements coverage, and 72% automated code coverage. Two minor defects were identi-
fied and resolved during the testing phase. These results confirm that the Khadamni plat-
form meets its defined functional and non-functional requirements and is functionally ready
for deployment, with clearly documented areas for improvement in AI scalability, horizon-
tal scaling, and automated end-to-end testing coverage.

General Conclusion

This report presented the complete design, development, and validation of Khadamni, an
integrated and intelligent service marketplace platform developed as part of a final-year en-
gineering internship at Satisfy Insight. The project was motivated by a clearly identified gap
in the current service platform landscape: the absence of a unified solution capable of com-
bining local and remote service discovery, real-time interaction, and intelligent automation
within a single coherent environment.

The first chapter established the project foundations by introducing the host company,
analyzing the limitations of existing platforms through a comparative study using the TRUST
evaluation method, and presenting the proposed solution along with the Agile development
methodology adopted. The second chapter formalized the system requirements, identify-
ing three actors, twenty-one functional requirements, and nine non-functional requirements,
supported by use case diagrams and interface wireframes. The third chapter addressed the
architectural design using the C4 modeling approach, progressively decomposing the sys-
tem across four levels of abstraction and applying Clean Architecture principles to ensure
maintainability and scalability. The fourth chapter detailed the technical implementation,
covering the technology stack, the development environment, and the integration of AI-
powered features including natural language request analysis and dispute mediation. Fi-
nally, the fifth chapter presented a comprehensive testing and validation strategy spanning
unit, functional, integration, security, and performance testing, achieving a 96.2% pass rate
across 52 test cases with 100% requirements coverage.

The resulting platform delivers several notable contributions. Khadamni successfully
unifies in-place and remote service providers within a single interface, a capability absent
from all existing solutions examined. The integration of artificial intelligence through the
Groq API enables users to express service needs in natural language, including Tunisian
dialect while the system automatically classifies requests and recommends providers. Real-
time communication, price negotiation, and live GPS tracking operate seamlessly through
WebSocket technology, providing an interactive and transparent user experience. The plat-
form’s moderation system, enhanced by AI-assisted dispute analysis, offers an efficient
mechanism for maintaining trust and accountability.

Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. The current deployment operates on a single-
machine architecture, which, as demonstrated by stress testing, begins to degrade beyond
approximately 600 concurrent users. The reliance on the free tier of the Groq API intro-
duces rate-limiting constraints that could affect availability under heavy usage. Addition-
ally, while the automated test suite covers backend logic, end-to-end testing remains man-
ual, and uploaded media files are stored locally rather than on a scalable cloud infrastruc-
ture.
